DAYLIGHT SAVING TIME

Each year in many countries, clocks are set forward in spring and back in autumn in an effort to save daylight hours. Like many modern practices, Daylight Saving Time (DST) dates back to ancient civilisations. Romans adjusted routines to the sun by using different scales in water clocks for different months.

The practice fell out of favour and was renewed in 1784 when Benjamin Franklin wrote a jocular article urging Paris residents to use daylight and reduce candle use. In 1895 New Zealand entomologist George Vernon Hudson proposed a biannual two-hour shift. Germany first pushed clocks forward in April 1916 to save fuel in World War I.

Over subsequent decades, DST use was sporadic and inconsistent. The UK and USA adopted it in both world wars but returned to standard time afterwards. In the USA, state and municipal choices caused confusion until the Uniform Time Act of 1966. Today over 70 countries use DST in some form, affecting around one sixth of the world population, but there is no uniform standard. Muslim countries often suspend it during Ramadan; the EU standardised DST in 2000; the USA adjusted it again in 2005.

Benefits are considerable and well documented. Extended evening daylight lets office workers take part in outdoor recreational activities after a 9-to-5 shift. It reduces domestic electricity consumption because sunlight replaces artificial lighting. Better-lit streets at peak hours reduce automobile accidents.

Tourism and hospitality gain increased revenue because people stay out later and spend more. Queensland, Australia did not implement DST after dairy farmers complained about disruption to milking schedules; the estimated annual economic drain is up to $4 billion.

Some research casts doubt on DST. When clocks are set back in autumn, pedestrians face a greater risk of being hit in the evening; safer early mornings do not offset it because fewer pedestrians are outside. Sleep loss after clock changes causes mood swings, reduced productivity, poorer physical well-being and fatigue. A 2008 Swedish study found heart-attack rates spike after the summer switch, and tiredness may contribute to road accidents.

Safety issues have also arisen in Latin America. Guatemala declined DST in 2008 because office workers would leave home while it was still dark; this natural cover for criminals was thought to increase incidents of street crime.

READING PASSAGE 2

THE LIFE OF SIR ISAAC NEWTON

A. Isaac Newton was born on January 4, 1643, in Lincolnshire, England. The son of a farmer who died before he was born, he spent early years with his maternal grandmother after his mother remarried. After a failed attempt to turn him into a farmer, he attended King's School and then Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661. When the Great Plague closed Cambridge in 1665, Newton returned home and formulated theories on calculus, light and colour; the farm was the setting for the supposed falling apple that inspired his work on gravity.

B. Newton returned to Cambridge in 1667. He constructed the first reflecting telescope in 1668, received his Master of Arts and became Professor of Mathematics. He demonstrated his telescope to the Royal Society and was elected to it. Fascinated with light, he published notes on optics. His experiments showed that white light was a composite of all colours and he asserted that light was composed of particles rather than waves. His methods were heavily criticised by Robert Hooke, leading to heated correspondence, a nervous breakdown and withdrawal from public life.

C. In 1684, astronomer Edmund Halley visited the reclusive Newton. Learning that Newton had calculated the elliptical paths of celestial bodies, Halley urged him to organise his notes. The 1687 Principia established the three laws of motion and universal gravity. It made Newton a star in intellectual circles and earned widespread acclaim as one of modern science's most important figures.

D. As an influential figure, Newton opposed King James II's attempts to reinstate Catholic teaching at universities and represented Cambridge in Parliament in 1689. He moved to London and became warden of the Royal Mint in 1696, then master in 1699.

E. Hooke's death in 1703 allowed Newton to become president of the Royal Society. The next year he published Opticks, based largely on earlier notes, detailing experiments with refraction, the colour spectrum, energy and electricity. Queen Anne knighted him in 1705.

F. A dispute erupted over Newton's claim to have originated calculus, the mathematical study of change. Newton had developed fluxions in the mid-1660s but left no public record. Gottfried Leibniz formulated and published his theories independently in 1684. Newton oversaw an investigation that favoured his own work, but later researchers concluded both men likely arrived at their ideas independently.

G. Newton was also obsessed with history and religious doctrines. Never married, he spent later years with his niece at Cranbury Park. He died on March 31, 1727 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His theories transformed understanding of the universe and his precise methodology helped give birth to the scientific method. Although Einstein later superseded parts of his theory, Newton's work remains a foundation of modern physics.

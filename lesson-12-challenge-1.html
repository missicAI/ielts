◉ Xem transcript

Head teacher: Hello, everyone, and welcome to our college Natural History Day. You have all got your programme, but let me give you some information about your options for this morning's sessions, which begin at half past nine. You need to attend one of these sessions.

Your first choice is called “Dogs Might Fly”, in Room 27. Professor Keenan, who ran a workshop last year on how dinosaurs became extinct, will lecture on the evolution of animals. In particular, she will look at how they may evolve in the future. This will be followed by a group discussion where you can ask questions and offer your own thoughts and opinions.

We all know animals communicate, but what about flowers? Your second choice is a video presentation called “Flowers Talk”. It considers the possibility that plants and flowers communicate with each other. Patrick Bell, who has just written a book on how plants adapt to their natural environment, presents it. It will take place here in the Main Hall; it was moved because the lecture room is being renovated.

The third choice is ideal for anyone wanting fresh air. We have called it “A World in Your Garden”, because it looks at things you can find by stepping out of your front door. Doctor Watkins will take you on a nature walk through the local park and tell you about fascinating animals and plants nearby.

The final option may not suit anyone frightened of snakes. It is a hands-on workshop where you can handle exotic creatures—not just snakes, but other reptiles too, including Tom Howard's tortoise Reggie, over 100 years old, and his lizard Arthur. Head to the Biology lab at 9.30. Remember to put on one of the white coats by the door before you enter.

◉ Xem transcript

Club speaker: Hello, I am delighted to welcome you to our Wildlife Club, and pleased that you are interested in the countryside and the plants and creatures of this area. You may be surprised at the variety here, even though we are not far from London. I will start with some nearby parks and open spaces.

One pleasant place is Halland Common. It has been public land for hundreds of years. The River Ouse, which flows into the sea eighty kilometres away, has its source in the common. There is an information board about plants and animals, and the common is accessible 24 hours a day.

Then there is Holt Island, noted for its great range of trees. In the past willows were grown commercially for basket-making, and this ancient craft has recently been reintroduced. The island is only open to the public from Friday to Sunday because it is small; if people were there every day, much of the wildlife would be kept away.

From there it is a short walk across the bridge to Longfield Country Park. Longfield has a modern replica of a farm from over two thousand years ago. Children's activities such as bread-making and face-painting are often arranged there. The park is open only during daylight hours, so bear that in mind if you visit.

READING READING PASSAGE 1

THE BALLPOINT PEN

Most of us have at least one, but how did this popular item evolve?

One morning in 1945, a crowd of 5,000 people jammed the entrance of Gimbels Department Store in New York. The day before, Gimbels had placed a full-page advertisement in the New York Times for a wonderful new invention, the ballpoint pen. The advertisement described the pen as “fantastic” and “miraculous”. Although they were expensive, $12.50 each, all 10,000 pens in stock were sold on the first day.

In fact, this “new” pen was not new at all. In 1888, John Loud, a leather manufacturer, had invented a pen with a reservoir of ink and a rolling ball. However, his pen was never produced, and efforts by other people to produce a commercially successful one failed too. The main problem was with the ink. If it was too thin, the ink leaked out of the pen. If it was too thick, it didn't come out of the pen at all.

Almost fifty years later, in 1935, a newspaper editor in Hungary thought he spent too much time filling his pens with ink. He decided to invent a better kind of pen. With the help of his brother, who was a chemist, he produced a ballpoint pen that didn't leak when the pen wasn't being used. The editor was called Ladislas Biro, and it was his name that people would associate more than any other with the ballpoint pen.

By chance, Biro met Augustine Justo, the Argentinian president. Justo was so impressed with Biro's invention that he invited him to set up a factory in Argentina. In 1943, the first Biro pens were produced. Unfortunately, they were not popular, since the pen needed to be held in a vertical position for the ink to come out. Biro redesigned the pen with a better ball, and in 1944 the new product was on sale throughout Argentina.

It was a North American, Milton Reynolds, who introduced the ballpoint pen to the USA. Copying Biro's design, he produced the version that sold so well at Gimbels. Another American, Patrick Frawley, improved the design and in 1950 began producing a pen he called the Papermate. It was an immediate success, and within a few years, Papermates were selling in their millions around the world.

READING PASSAGE 2

LAND OF THE RISING SUN

A. Japan has a significantly better record in terms of average mathematical attainment than England and Wales. International comparisons since the 1960s established that Japanese pupils aged 13 had better average scores and that England had a larger proportion of low attainers and much greater variation. The percentage of GNP spent on education is reasonably similar, so how is the higher and more consistent attainment achieved?

B. Lower secondary schools in Japan cover three years, from grade seven to grade nine. Virtually all pupils attend state schools. Schools are modern, spacious and set back from roads. Lessons last a standardised 50 minutes followed by a ten-minute break. Teachers begin with a formal address and mutual bowing, then concentrate on whole-class teaching. Classes of about 40 are unstreamed. Pupils stay together for all lessons and develop strong class identity and loyalty.

C. Traditional ways of teaching form the basis of lessons, and remarkably quiet classes take their own notes. Everyone has a copy of the textbook supplied by the central education authority, Monbusho. Textbooks are small and inexpensive but well set out and logically developed. Besides approving textbooks, Monbusho decides the highly centralised national curriculum and how it is delivered.

D. Lessons follow the same pattern. Pupils put homework solutions on the board; teachers comment, correct or elaborate; pupils mark their own work so they can see why they made mistakes. The teacher then explains the new topic slowly with repetition and elaboration. Examples are demonstrated and textbook questions are worked through with the class before individual practice. Supplementary worksheets are rare because logical textbooks and relatively homogeneous classes make them unnecessary.

E. It is remarkable that large mixed-ability classes stay together for maths throughout compulsory schooling. Teachers give individual help after lessons or school and set extra work. Strugglers receive help from teachers or neighbours. Parents are kept informed, help children keep up and may send them to Juku private evening tuition. The attitude is that if you work hard enough, you can do almost anything.

F. Major contributing factors include the high value placed on education, maths as an important compulsory subject, hard work and accuracy. Classes support slower pupils, competition within classes is limited, and pupils focus positively on learning for themselves and improving their own standard. The stereotype of boring repetition may be unfair: observed lessons were mainly good and some inspirational.

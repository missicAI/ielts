Exercise 2

◉ Xem transcript

Researcher: Hello, Joe, good to see you again.

Joe: Hi, you too.

Researcher: So how did you get on with the devices we asked you to test for us?

Joe: Oh, fine. Well, mostly.

Researcher: We will come back to those in a minute. First, I was wondering if I could ask you a few questions about your attitude to new electrical products. This will help us with future marketing. How much do you spend on electronic items a month?

Joe: I do not earn a lot, so I do not have much left after paying rent, bills, food and so on. Anything else is a luxury. I would guess about five, maybe ten per cent of my monthly salary.

Researcher: What influences you in your choice? Say you wanted a new mobile phone—how would you decide which one to buy?

Joe: First I look at reviews on the Internet, what other customers think. Then I ask my friends. Their opinions are probably more important than anything.

Researcher: How much does advertising help you choose a product?

Joe: It depends how it is advertised and who is advertising it. If it is someone I respect, like a famous sportsman or actor, that can certainly make a difference.

Researcher: And where do you buy most products? The High Street? The Internet?

Joe: Most people avoid shops because they think things are cheaper online. But if you tell a shop assistant that a camera is £100 on the Internet, they will often match the price. So that is where I go. You get personal service and do not have to wait for delivery. Ordering online can mean waiting for ages. I hate that; I am very impatient.

Researcher: Do you ever see a product and think, “I have absolutely got to get one of those”?

Joe: All the time, especially when I walk past a shop and see a new electronic item in the window, particularly if it is unusual—something I have never seen before. It takes a lot of willpower.

Exercise 3

◉ Xem transcript

Researcher: Right, Joe, let us move on. We gave you three items to test. Let us start with the mobile phone.

Joe: It has good points and bad points. The purple and silver make it quite eye-catching—modern and exciting. It is very small, perhaps too small. With big hands you can press one key and another at the same time. On the other hand, thanks to the oval design it fits comfortably in your hand; that is better than the usual rectangular shape.

When I sent text messages, I had real problems seeing what I was writing. The screen was not too small, just rather dark, and outside you could hardly see anything. The warning noise it makes when you move away from it is a good idea but really annoying; after a while it would drive me mad.

I do not want a phone that is difficult to operate. There is no point having one that looks good if making a call or sending a message takes ages. I want one that does not have problems picking up a signal or cut you off halfway through a call. I cannot see the point of all the games and extra things.

Researcher: Next, the digital radio. What did you think?

Joe: The audio quality was crisp and clear, even at high volume. Some systems sound distorted at higher volumes, but not this one. The problem was that the choice of stations seemed very limited. Moving the aerial or putting the radio high or low made no difference. There was also a delay when you turned up the volume: you pressed the control and nothing happened for a few seconds. The same happened when changing stations.

Researcher: The third item was the laptop.

Joe: I really liked it. It is small and compact but easy to use; I do not think you could make it smaller. But at £900 I am not sure you would get many customers. Bring it down to about £400. The memory is fine—three hundred gigabytes is more than enough for most people. The way the keyboard folds out to full size is clever. But there is nowhere to play CD-ROMs, and I would include a light in the keyboard so it can be used in the dark.

Exercise 4

◉ Xem transcript

Hello, and thank you for asking me to your teachers' meeting to talk about the Dinosaur Museum and your students' visit. Let me give you some basic information first. We are open every day from 9.00 a.m. to 8.00 p.m., except on Mondays when we close at 1.30 p.m. The only day of the year when we are closed is 25 December. You can book a guided tour for your school group whenever we are open.

When you arrive with a school group, remain with your group in the car park. One or more guides will welcome you and brief you there because our entrance is quite small and there is not enough room to brief groups in the exhibition area.

Allow a minimum of 90 minutes: 15 minutes to get on and off the coach, 45 minutes for the guided tour, and 30 minutes for after-tour activities. If you have lunch at the museum, allow more time. There are two cafés with seating for 80 people; reserve seats because they get crowded at lunchtime. Outside at the back there are tables where students can bring their own lunch and eat in the open air.

Exercise 5

◉ Xem transcript

When students enter the museum foyer, we ask them to check backpacks, books and lunch boxes into the cloakroom before entering the museum proper. This is a strict rule because things have gone missing after school visits and some exhibits are fragile and could be knocked accidentally.

We provide school students with handouts containing questions and quizzes. There is so much to learn, and it is fun to have something to do, so they need to bring something to write with. We allow students to take photographs. For project work, a visual record of what they see is useful to add to reports. They must not bring food or drinks of any kind into the museum.

Exercise 6

◉ Xem transcript

There are a few things students can do after the tour. In the theatrette on the ground floor there are continuous screenings of short documentaries about dinosaurs which they can see at any time.

We used to have an activity room with interactive things such as making dinosaur models, drawing and painting pictures, and even hunting for dinosaur eggs. Unfortunately, it was damaged in a bad storm when water came through the roof, so it is closed at the moment.

We do have an IT centre where students have access to CD-ROMs with a range of dinosaur games. These are fun, but they also teach about dinosaurs' lives—how they found food, protected their habitat and survived threats. Please feel free to ask if you would like to know anything else.

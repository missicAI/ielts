READING PASSAGE 1

PEDESTRIANS ONLY

How traffic-free shopping streets developed

A. The concept of traffic-free shopping areas goes back a long time. During the Middle Ages, souks were built in Middle Eastern countries so people could shop in comfort and safety. As far back as 2,000 years ago, road traffic was banned from central Rome during the day to allow free movement of pedestrians. Elsewhere pedestrians shared streets with horses, coaches and, later, motorised vehicles.

B. The modern traffic-free shopping street was born in Europe in the 1960s, when city populations and car ownership increased rapidly. Dirty exhaust fumes and the risks of crossing roads made shopping unpleasant and dangerous. Many believed it was time to experiment with car-free streets.

C. At first shopkeepers resisted. They believed people would avoid streets they could not reach by car. The first European closures even produced noisy demonstrations and predictions of lost customers.

D. Research later revealed unexpected statistics. Visitors rose by 50 per cent in Munich, Cologne and Hamburg; Copenhagen's main shopping street reported sales increases of 25–40 per cent. Minneapolis shopkeepers were so impressed that they offered to pay construction and maintenance costs.

E. Clothing, food and small-luxury shops prospered, but furniture and large-appliance stores saw sales drop and often moved to out-of-town retail zones with car parks and facilities.

READING PASSAGE 2

AIR TRAFFIC CONTROL IN THE USA

A. A 1956 collision over the Grand Canyon led to creation of the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which regulates and oversees increasingly congested US skies. The resulting air-traffic-control structure greatly improved flight safety.

B. Rudimentary ATC existed by the 1920s: controllers used lights and flags near airports, while beacons marked cross-country airways. Radio and radar later enabled controllers to follow and communicate with aircraft over wider areas.

C. Many people imagine ATC simply tracks every aircraft, but different flights and areas require different levels of control. The system balances safety with practical freedom for aviation.

D. Regulations divide the sky by altitude and operating environment so aircraft with different performance can share airspace safely.

E. FAA rules distinguish commercial, private and recreational pilots and impose different permissions according to aircraft, route and pilot qualifications.

F. In good weather pilots may use Visual Flight Rules; poor visibility requires Instrument Flight Rules and an instrument rating. Regulations allow VFR and IFR operations in the same controlled airspace.

G. Airspace is designated Classes A–F. Class E contains much general aviation below 5,490m; Class A above that altitude is IFR. Classes B, C and D govern airports with increasingly rigorous rules.
